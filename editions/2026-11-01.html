The big read

Wall Street had its worst day since June, and Intel its worst since 1974

Worries that the AI spending boom won't pay off hit stocks hard on Friday, Oct. 30. The S&P 500 fell 1.8%, its worst day since June, and the Nasdaq dropped 2.9%, AP reported.

Intel sank 28.6% after a weak forecast, its worst day since 1974. Nvidia, Broadcom and Qualcomm slid too, and Amazon fell after reporting $42.4 billion in negative free cash flow over 12 months.

It capped a third straight losing week for the S&P 500.

A judge ended Trump's lawsuit against The Wall Street Journal for good

A federal judge in Miami on Oct. 31 permanently dismissed President Trump's defamation suit against The Wall Street Journal and Rupert Murdoch. It cannot be refiled.

The case, which sought $10 billion, centered on the Journal's report about a letter said to bear Trump's name in a 2003 birthday album for Jeffrey Epstein. Trump denies writing it.

Judge Darrin Gayles ruled the complaint did not show "actual malice," the bar public figures must clear. A Trump spokesperson said he will press forward, and an appeal is expected.

Ukraine gets the green light to buy 500 Patriot interceptors

The State Department on Oct. 30 approved a possible $2.1 billion sale of 500 PAC 3 MSE missiles to Ukraine. It is the most advanced Patriot interceptor and one of the few weapons that can stop ballistic missiles.

Ukraine will pay with funds from Canada, Germany and Norway plus an EU loan. No delivery date was given, and President Zelensky warned it could take years. Ukraine's supply of these interceptors ran out earlier in October.

Milwaukee threw its first World Series parade

Brewers fans packed downtown Milwaukee on Oct. 30 for the franchise's first World Series championship parade, with many arriving hours early.

The Brewers clinched the title two days earlier with a 4 to 1 win over the Tampa Bay Rays, taking the series in five games. The parade rolled down Wisconsin Avenue, and Milwaukee Public Schools canceled classes for the day.

Deep dive

Appeals court again refuses to let DOJ set aside 20,000 California ballots

What happened. A federal appeals panel voted 2 to 1 on Oct. 30 to reject a Justice Department request involving California ballots. The department wanted the state to set aside ballots sent to about 20,000 people it says may not be citizens. A district judge had denied the same request on Oct. 28. It was the appeals court's second rejection in under a week.

Why it matters. The ruling came days before the Nov. 3 midterms, after millions of Californians had already voted. The majority found "no feasible way" to identify and separate the ballots of specific voters. An analysis filed Oct. 31 found 8,880 names on the federal list, or 42%, were not in any current California voter file.

What each side says. Judges Mark Bennett and Gabriel Sanchez formed the majority, saying the request would undermine the Constitution's Elections Clause. Judge Patrick Bumatay dissented, writing that "California says it can do nothing to prevent illegal voting." A Justice Department spokesperson said each ineligible ballot "cancels out the vote of an American citizen." California officials call the federal list highly unreliable.

What comes next. The Justice Department has said it will appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court. Separately, Judge Trina Thompson barred the department from pressuring election workers to stop processing ballots. Another judge ordered the government to share its list with outside lawyers, and the government appealed that order.

Daily trivia

The judge in Trump's case against The Wall Street Journal applied the "actual malice" standard. Which 1964 Supreme Court case created it?

B. New York Times Co. v. Sullivan. The 1964 ruling says public officials must prove a publisher knew a statement was false or showed reckless disregard for the truth.
